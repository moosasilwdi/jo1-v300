The story

        
Jo-1 is a protein every one of your cells needs. In some people the
        immune system turns on it, and the muscles and the lungs pay for it.

        
The whole thing starts in a groove. One HLA molecule picks up a short
        piece of Jo-1, fifteen letters long, and holds it up for a T cell to read. Which piece gets
        picked decides everything that follows.

        
So that is the question this app was built around — and it was
        built by a designer, on a phone, because it turns out a phone can answer it.

      

      

        
Why this method

        
JO1 was built by Moosa Silwadi, an architect and designer,
        after his own diagnosis of anti-Jo-1 antisynthetase syndrome in 2026. Not a laboratory
        project — one person reading, asking, and checking.

        
The reading kept arriving at the same fork. One road quiets the whole
        immune system: it works, and it costs you your ordinary defences against germs. The other road
        goes after one thing only — the single groove where this disease is ordered.
        A key for one lock, instead of cutting the power to the whole building.

        
That is the method JO1 was built for. It does not treat anything
        and it does not design a medicine. It narrows: hundreds of possible slices down to the few
        worth a laboratory's time, and it refuses the ones that would grip everybody else's holder too.

        
Two attempts at this idea reached trial years ago and both were stopped,
        because a peptide that sits in the groove can wake the cells it was meant to quiet. That is in
        the Warning panel below, and it is the reason the app narrows carefully instead of promising
        anything.

      

      

        
What makes it useful

        
The established tools score a piece against one HLA type. That
        is the right first question, and they answer it well.

        
This app asks the second one: does the same piece also grip
        everybody else's holder? A piece that grips many types would blunt normal defence against
        germs — so a tight score on its own is not good news. It is a warning sign waiting to be
        checked.

        
117 holders, every one of them, in about two seconds, in your own browser.
        That check is the app.

      

      

        
What is new here

        

          
The 117 check is the main move

          
Not a feature buried in a menu. It is the button under the map, and the whole app is
          arranged around it.

          
Narrowing turns the list upside down

          
Rank the top 20 by how few other people they grip and the order reverses: the tightest
          piece is often the worst candidate. That reversal is the finding, and it is the thing you
          cannot see by scoring one holder at a time.

          
It runs on your phone, not on a server

          
The trained networks are inside the page. No upload, no account, no waiting. Your
          sequence never leaves your phone.

          
One engine, two languages

          
Science gives you the nanomolar numbers. Abstract gives you the same answer in plain
          words. One switch, same engine underneath.

          
The groove is a real picture

          
Built on measured coordinates from PDB 1A6A, with your own letters laid along the
          measured path — turn it, slide it, open it full screen. Not a cartoon drawn to look
          convincing.

        

      

      

      

      

        
Questions

        

          
Is this medical advice?

          
No. It is a research filter. It cannot diagnose anything and it cannot tell you anything
          about your own body.

          
Do I need to know my HLA type?

          
No. Leave DRB1*03:01 — the type linked to anti-Jo-1 — and the app works.

          
Where do the numbers come from?

          
The MHCnuggets class II networks, trained on measured binding data and published with
          their paper. They are predictions, not measurements.

          
Is anything I paste sent anywhere?

          
No. Everything runs inside the page on your device.

          
Why 117 and not more?

          
117 is how many class II holders have a trained network in that release. More types
          exist; they have no model yet.

          
Can I put in any protein?

          
Yes. Paste a sequence or drop a FASTA file and the whole thing is cut into fifteen-letter
          pieces and scored.

          
What counts as a good result?

          
Tight on your holder and loose on the other 116. Tight on everything is the result
          that should stop you.

          
Does this design a medicine?

          
No. It narrows. It takes hundreds of possibilities down to a handful worth a laboratory's
          time — and the Warning panel below says what happened the last two times this idea
          went to trial.

        

      

      

      

      

        
How to use it

        

          
1 · Holder

          
Pick whose HLA type you are testing. Every number in the app depends on this one choice.
          If you do not know yours, leave DRB1*03:01 — the type linked to anti-Jo-1.

          
2 · Protein

          
Paste a protein, drop a FASTA file, or press Load Jo-1. Press Scan and every
          window along it is scored.

          
3 · Results

          
The map draws grip strength along the whole protein; peaks are the slices that grip.
          Drag across it, or use ◀ ▶ to step one slice at a time and
          + − to zoom in. Then press Check this slice against all 117 types —
          that is the answer worth having.

          
4 · Groove

          
The selected slice drawn in the holder's groove, with the four pockets marked.

        

      

      

      

      

        
Reading the answer

        
Every piece gets one of four words for how hard the
        holder grips it: very tight, tight, moderate, loose.

        
After the 117 check it also says how many other people's
        holders grip the same piece. None is the good answer. Three or more is the
        answer that should stop you.

      

      

        
Reading the numbers

        
Strength is in nanomolar (nM). Lower is tighter.
        Under 50 is very tight, under 500 tight, under 5 000 moderate, above that loose.

        
The 117-type check returns one of three verdicts.
        Selective — it grips your holder and nothing else tightly.
        Mostly selective — one or two others.
        Not selective — three or more, which is the result that should stop you.

      

      

        
What the number is

        
Tightness is only half the question. A peptide that grips many HLA types would also blunt normal defence against germs. The button under a selected slice scores it against all 117 networks. Across the 61 Jo-1 decoys this reorders the list: the tightest are often the least selective.

        
A predicted binding strength in nanomolar from the MHCnuggets network for that HLA type, trained on measured binding data. It is a prediction, not a measurement. A lab would also run NetMHCIIpan and then test in cells. Treat a result here as a shortlist.

      

      

      

      

        
Warning — this strategy has harmed patients

        
Peptides designed to occupy the same groove have gone to trial in multiple sclerosis, and both were stopped:

        
    
          
  • CGP77116 — halted after 8 patients; 3 had disease flares caused by the treatment, with a rise in reactive T cells in blood and spinal fluid.
    • 
          
  • NBI-5788 — suspended after hypersensitivity reactions in 9% of patients.
    • 
        

        
A peptide that binds the groove can wake the very cells it was meant to quiet.

Pockets of the selected holder

        

        
Read from the IPD-IMGT/HLA protein alignments, release 3.66.0. These are the polymorphic β-chain positions that line each pocket: P1 = β85, β86; P4 = β13, β70, β71, β74; P6 = β11, β30; P9 = β9, β57. For DQ and DP only the β chain is shown; the α chain also contributes.

      

      

        
What the DR3 pockets prefer

        

          
P1
L I V M > F Y. Val85 + Val86 make it a smaller pocket than other DR types. Charged, G, P excluded.

          
P4
D strongly, then E. Lys71 + Arg74 make it electropositive, so K and R are shut out.

          
P6
K R H. Electronegative. Small polar residues also sit here and leave P9 open.

          
P9
No published residue list for DR3. Electronegative (Glu9, Asp57), so acidic disfavoured.

        

        
Geluk 1994 gives two alternative submotifs. This rule-based reading is a cross-check on the network; across 285 Jo-1 variants the two agree only weakly (Spearman 0.17), so the network is what the ranking uses.
